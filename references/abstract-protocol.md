# Abstract Construction Protocol

Use this protocol when drafting, revising, or compressing an academic abstract.

## Format Authority

Apply requirements in this order:

1. target-journal instructions;
2. article-type conventions;
3. user-specified constraints;
4. the defaults in this protocol.

When no journal requirement is available, write an unstructured English abstract of approximately 180–250 words. Prefer the shortest version that preserves the objective, essential method, decisive findings, and bounded conclusion.

Use visible headings only when the journal requests a structured abstract. Required headings may differ from the five functional components; map the functions into the journal's headings without duplicating content.

## Five Functional Components

### Background/Problem

Use one or two concise sentences to establish the specific problem and why it matters. Do not reproduce the Introduction's literature review or begin with a universal statement.

### Objective/Purpose

State the primary aim in one direct sentence. Use an action that matches the actual paper: evaluate, compare, characterize, test, synthesize, develop, or validate.

### Methods/Approach

Include only details necessary to interpret the findings:

- study or review design;
- population, system, dataset, corpus, or material;
- key conditions or comparison;
- primary method or analytical logic.

Omit procedural detail that does not change interpretation. For reviews, state the search or corpus boundary and synthesis approach only when supported by the manuscript.

Explain which approach addresses which part of the research problem. Retain method names needed to identify the approach; give each a clear role rather than listing techniques or abbreviations.

### Results/Main Findings

Give the largest share of the word budget to the decisive findings. Use verified quantitative values when they materially improve interpretation. Preserve direction, comparator, units, uncertainty, and denominators where needed to avoid distortion.

Do not replace results with statements such as “important findings were obtained.” Do not report statistical significance without the corresponding comparison or result context.

### Conclusion/Implications

State what the findings support within the study boundary. Include the principal scientific, methodological, or practical implication only when it follows from the results. Avoid repeating the objective or introducing an unsupported recommendation.

End the abstract with the paper's value. Specify what the findings change in understanding, enable methodologically, or support in practical decisions, and connect this contribution to the opening problem. Use only the value demonstrated by the inspected manuscript. A generic claim of importance, a restated objective, or a future-work wish does not communicate that value. For a protocol, proposal, or incomplete study, label the contribution as expected.

## Result Selection and Organization

Before drafting, express the paper's main finding in one sentence: what does this study let the reader know? For each candidate result, identify the research question it answers, its inspected evidence source, and its role in supporting the main finding. This is an internal selection step, not an additional required deliverable.

Prioritize results that:

- directly answer the primary research question;
- provide indispensable evidence for the central contribution;
- reveal a consequential relationship, difference, pattern, trade-off, or condition;
- change scientific interpretation or a relevant decision.

Numerical magnitude, statistical significance, or first-place model ranking alone does not establish abstract priority. If removing a result leaves the main finding fully supported and accurately bounded, treat it as a compression candidate. Retain a negative finding or applicability condition when its omission would materially change the conclusion.

Organize selected results in the order needed to understand the discovery:

1. **Main finding:** answer the central research question directly.
2. **Decisive support:** give the most informative comparison or quantitative evidence, with the comparator, measure, and conditions needed to interpret it.
3. **Condition or further understanding:** state a supported boundary, trade-off, or explanation that changes how the main finding is understood. Claim a mechanism only when the evidence establishes it.

Use only the parts needed for the paper; this sequence is not a three-result quota. Select by explanatory contribution rather than figure order or the number of analyses performed.

Each Results sentence should express a substantive observation. Terms identify what was studied; numbers substantiate the observation. Avoid substituting a sequence of method names, metric names, experimental factors, or isolated values for a finding.

As a diagnostic, temporarily remove numbers and abbreviations. The sentence should still communicate a specific relationship, difference, pattern, or change. If it reduces to “good performance” or “important results,” identify the substantive finding before restoring its supporting evidence. This check does not justify dropping decisive quantitative evidence from the final abstract.

Illustration only, with no empirical claim intended:

- Inventory: “Accuracy, recall, F1, and AUC were evaluated across label budgets, noise levels, and feature combinations.”
- Finding: “Under limited labeling, the proposed method retained leak-detection sensitivity, with its advantage over the baseline increasing as the label budget decreased.” Add the verified comparison that best supports this statement when the manuscript establishes it.

## Article-Type Routing

### Empirical Study

Use Background → Objective → Methods → Results → Conclusion. The abstract must report actual findings rather than only describing the study design.

### Review Paper

Use Background → Review objective and scope → Search or synthesis method → Main synthesis findings → Implications. Distinguish corpus patterns from author interpretation. Do not claim systematic or comprehensive coverage unless the documented search supports it.

### Methods or Framework Paper

Use Problem → Objective → Proposed method or framework → Validation evidence → Added capability and boundary. A proposed framework alone is not a validated improvement.

### Protocol, Proposal, or Incomplete Study

Use Background → Objective → Planned methods → Expected analytical or practical contribution. Do not include a Results component or prediction phrased as a completed finding. Label the output so readers cannot mistake it for a completed study.

## Insight Selection

Extract zero to three central insights after identifying the manuscript's primary question and decisive findings. Most complete papers should need one or two.

An insight qualifies when it:

- directly answers the main research question;
- connects two or more material findings into a defensible synthesis;
- establishes an important boundary or distinction;
- explains what the results change in scientific interpretation or decision use.

An item does not qualify merely because it appears in Discussion. Exclude speculative mechanisms, secondary literature debates, minor subgroup observations, long lists of implications, and recommendations not demonstrated by the study.

For every candidate insight, ask:

1. Which specific result or result combination supports it?
2. Does the manuscript's Discussion or Conclusion state the same interpretation within comparable limits?
3. What qualification would change its meaning if omitted?
4. Is it more important than the other candidate insights?

Keep only the highest-value candidates. Integrate them into the Results and Conclusion sentences rather than listing them separately.

## Word-Budget Guidance

When no journal format dictates allocation, use the approximate balance below as a diagnostic rather than a quota:

- Background and objective: 15–25%;
- Methods: 15–25%;
- Results and central insights: 40–55%;
- Conclusion and implication: 10–20%.

Cut in this order when compressing:

1. generic background;
2. low-value method detail;
3. secondary findings and Discussion branches;
4. repeated implications;
5. stylistic redundancy.

Do not cut a qualification that prevents overstatement, a denominator needed to interpret a number, or the principal result merely to preserve background prose.

## Tense and Voice

- Use present tense for established context and the paper's current conclusion.
- Use past tense for study-specific procedures and observations unless field convention strongly prefers another tense.
- Prefer direct constructions and concrete verbs.
- Avoid promotional adjectives, throat-clearing openings, and claims of novelty unsupported by a bounded search.

## Abbreviations and Keywords

Define abbreviations on first use unless universally conventional in the field. Avoid abbreviations used only once.

Follow the journal's keyword count and vocabulary rules. Otherwise provide approximately four to six English keywords that improve discoverability, mix broad and specific concepts, and do not merely repeat every word in the title.

## Optional Chinese or Bilingual Output

Generate Chinese only when explicitly requested. In bilingual mode:

- compose each language independently;
- preserve the same methods, quantities, findings, insights, qualifications, and conclusion strength;
- allow natural sentence restructuring;
- verify that neither version adds information absent from the other or from the manuscript.
